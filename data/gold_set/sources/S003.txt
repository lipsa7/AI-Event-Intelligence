Nigeria can cut construction costs through local production - Chime
August 9, 2026 12:00 am
By Anozie Egole
The Chief Executive Officer of Elan Exhibitions West Africa Limited and Convener of the Mega Project Nigeria Expo, Jude Chime, who also chairs the Nigerian Association of Chambers of Commerce, Industry, Mines and Agriculture Trade Fair Committee, speaks with ANOZIE EGOLE on the challenges facing Nigeria's construction sector and the policy interventions required to accelerate sustainable growth and infrastructure development
How have persistent inflation and exchange rate volatility impacted construction costs, project delivery and profitability in Nigeria?
There's no doubt the impact has been significant. If you speak to any contractor, developer or supplier today, they'll tell you that the cost of doing business has changed dramatically over the past two years.
We've seen sharp increases in the prices of reinforcement steel, electrical cables, aluminium products, imported elevators, HVAC systems, glazing materials and many other essential construction components. In some cases, quotations prepared at the beginning of a project are no longer valid by the time procurement starts because prices have moved so quickly. That creates uncertainty for both contractors and clients.
Many projects have had to be reviewed or delayed as developers work to accommodate higher construction costs. Fixed-price contracts have become particularly difficult because contractors often have to absorb unexpected increases in material prices, which puts pressure on profitability.
What we're also seeing is that international manufacturers are requesting payments in foreign currency or much shorter payment terms than before, adding another layer of pressure on Nigerian businesses.
The positive side is that these challenges are encouraging companies to become more innovative. More firms are looking at local alternatives where quality standards permit, strengthening procurement planning, negotiating more flexible contract structures and improving financial risk management. I also believe this is an opportunity for Nigeria to accelerate local manufacturing of construction materials so we are less exposed to external shocks in the future.
Economic reforms are never easy, especially in the early stages. We've seen reforms such as the removal of fuel subsidies, the liberalisation of the foreign exchange market and ongoing tax reforms create immediate pressures for businesses, particularly those in construction where operating costs are already high.
The reality is that these reforms have increased the cost of transportation, logistics, imported construction materials and project financing. Naturally, that has affected contractors, developers and manufacturers across the industry.
That said, I also believe reforms should be viewed from a long-term perspective. If they are implemented consistently and supported with the right policies, they can improve transparency, strengthen investor confidence and create a more stable business environment.
Where I think government needs to do more is in supporting productive sectors like construction and manufacturing during this transition. Infrastructure development remains one of the strongest drivers of economic growth. So, policies that encourage investment, provide concessionary financing, offer tax incentives for local manufacturers and simplify regulatory processes will help businesses adjust while keeping projects moving.
Ultimately, the success of these reforms will not only be measured by economic indicators but by how they translate into increased investments, more completed infrastructure projects and sustainable job creation.
As a key indicator of economic performance, what does the current state of Nigeria's construction industry reveal about the broader economy?
Construction has always reflected the health of an economy. When infrastructure projects are increasing, housing developments are expanding and private investment is growing, it's usually a sign that the economy is moving in the right direction.
In Nigeria today, the demand is certainly there. Our housing deficit remains significant, our population continues to grow, urbanisation is accelerating and there's a clear need for better roads, housing, healthcare facilities, schools, industrial parks and other critical infrastructure.
What has changed is not the demand but the pace at which projects are being delivered. Higher financing costs, inflation and exchange rate volatility have made project execution more challenging for both the public and private sectors.
Despite these challenges, I remain optimistic. We're still seeing major infrastructure investments by government and increased interest from private investors. With greater macroeconomic stability, more affordable long-term financing and stronger collaboration between government and the private sector, I believe the construction industry can become one of the biggest drivers of Nigeria's economic growth over the next decade.
Delayed payments on government contracts remain a major concern for contractors. How serious is the problem, and what reforms are needed to address it?
Delayed payments remain one of the biggest challenges facing the construction industry, and it's an issue that affects businesses of all sizes. When contractors complete certified stages of a project but have to wait months before receiving payment, it creates a ripple effect across the entire value chain.
Contractors still have obligations to meet. They have to pay staff salaries, settle suppliers, maintain equipment, service bank loans and keep projects running. When payments are delayed, cash flow becomes strained, projects slow down, and in some cases, contractors are forced to borrow at high interest rates just to continue working. That ultimately increases the overall cost of delivering infrastructure.
From my perspective, improving the payment process would have a significant positive impact on the industry. Government can strengthen payment certification systems, encourage prompt payment practices and explore mechanisms such as project escrow accounts, payment guarantees or dedicated project bank accounts to ensure certified work is paid for within agreed timelines.
We've seen similar approaches adopted successfully in other markets to improve contractor confidence and reduce project delays. As Nigeria continues to invest in infrastructure, ensuring timely payment to contractors will not only improve project delivery but also encourage more private sector participation and create a healthier business environment for everyone involved.
How has Nigeria's current tax and regulatory framework affected the competitiveness of construction firms, and what reforms are needed to improve the business environment?
Every business understands the importance of regulation and taxation, especially when they support national development. However, the business environment is most effective when regulations are clear, predictable and efficient.
One of the challenges many companies in the construction sector face today is navigating multiple taxes, levies and regulatory requirements across different levels of government. Developers and contractors often have to obtain approvals from planning authorities, environmental agencies, fire services, utility providers and local government authorities before a project can commence. While these approvals are necessary, the processes can sometimes be lengthy, overlapping and costly.
The result is increased project timelines, higher compliance costs and, ultimately, additional expenses that are passed on to investors, developers and end users. For businesses looking to invest or expand, predictability and efficiency are just as important as the cost of doing business.
I believe there is a great opportunity to simplify these processes through greater collaboration between government agencies and increased digitalisation. A more streamlined approval system, harmonised taxes and transparent regulatory procedures would significantly improve Nigeria's ease of doing business, attract more local and foreign investment, and enable infrastructure projects to move from planning to execution much faster.
At the end of the day, creating a more business-friendly regulatory environment is not just about supporting companies, it's about encouraging investment, accelerating development and strengthening the country's overall economic competitiveness.
What innovative funding models should the government adopt to bridge infrastructure financing gap?
One thing I have always said is that government cannot finance Nigeria's infrastructure needs alone. When you consider the scale of investment required for roads, railways, housing, power, water infrastructure, ports and logistics hubs, it's clear that a more collaborative approach is needed.
The good news is that we already have examples of financing models that can be expanded. Public-Private Partnerships have delivered important infrastructure projects in Nigeria, while some institutions are helping to create new opportunities for long-term infrastructure financing. These are encouraging developments that should continue to be strengthened.
I also believe we need to unlock more domestic capital by creating avenues for pension funds, insurance companies, infrastructure bonds, private equity firms and development finance institutions to participate in financing bankable infrastructure projects. Around the world, these institutions play a significant role in funding long-term development because infrastructure is a long-term investment that requires patient capital.
Another area with enormous potential is project preparation. Investors are more willing to commit capital when projects are well-structured, transparent and commercially viable. Strengthening feasibility studies, procurement processes and risk-sharing frameworks will improve investor confidence and attract more private sector participation.
This is exactly why conversations like those we will be having at Mega Project Nigeria 2026 are so important. Bringing together government, financiers, development institutions, investors and the private sector creates the right platform to discuss practical financing solutions and build partnerships that can move projects from concept to execution.
Ultimately, closing Nigeria's infrastructure gap will require more than public funding. It will require innovative financing, strong institutions and sustained collaboration between government, development partners and the private sector. I believe that's the path to delivering the quality infrastructure our growing economy needs.
How can Nigeria's construction industry help create jobs, deepen local content and foster more inclusive economic growth?
I often describe construction as one of the most powerful engines of economic growth because very few industries create opportunities across as many sectors as construction does. Every major project, whether it's a housing estate, a highway, a hospital, a factory or a commercial development, creates employment and supports thousands of livelihoods.
When a project begins, the opportunities go far beyond contractors and engineers. It creates work for architects, builders, quantity surveyors, artisans, welders, electricians, plumbers, painters, transport operators, equipment suppliers, manufacturers, logistics companies and countless small businesses that supply goods and services throughout the construction process. That's why construction has one of the highest employment multiplier effects of any sector.

It's also a major driver of local content. The more we encourage the use of locally manufactured cement, steel, glass, aluminium products, ceramic tiles, paints, cables, furniture and other building materials, the more we strengthen Nigerian industries, reduce dependence on imports and create sustainable jobs across the manufacturing value chain.
I believe construction has a unique role to play in achieving inclusive economic growth because its impact reaches every part of society. It creates opportunities for skilled professionals, artisans, young graduates, SMEs and local communities. When infrastructure expands, businesses grow, investments increase and communities become more connected and productive.
This is one of the reasons Mega Project Nigeria remains so important. Beyond showcasing products and technologies, it brings together everyone involved in the construction ecosystem, from manufacturers and developers to policymakers, financiers and technology providers, to explore practical partnerships that will drive investment, create jobs and accelerate sustainable development.
Ultimately, investing in construction is not just about building physical infrastructure; it's about building industries, creating employment and laying the foundation for long-term economic prosperity.
How are insecurity, logistics challenges and unreliable power supply impacting infrastructure development and project delivery in Nigeria?
These three issues - insecurity, logistics and power supply - have become some of the biggest cost drivers for infrastructure projects in Nigeria today. Even when funding is available and projects are ready to commence, these operational challenges can significantly affect delivery timelines and overall project costs.
Take logistics, for example. Moving heavy construction equipment or building materials from the ports to project sites can be time-consuming and expensive due to congestion, poor road conditions and transportation delays. For projects outside major cities, the challenges can be even greater, and every delay ultimately adds to the cost of construction.
Power is another major concern. Many construction sites, manufacturing plants and industrial facilities still rely heavily on diesel-powered generators because of inconsistent electricity supply. That increases operating costs for manufacturers of building materials as well as contractors executing projects on-site. Those additional costs are eventually reflected in the final cost of infrastructure.
Security is equally important. A safe and stable environment gives investors the confidence to commit long-term capital and allows projects to progress without unnecessary disruptions. Where there are security concerns, contractors often face additional expenses for site protection, insurance and logistics, all of which affect project budgets and delivery schedules.
The encouraging thing is that these are challenges we can address through sustained investment in transportation infrastructure, improved power supply, stronger security coordination and more efficient supply chains. As these fundamentals improve, we'll see lower construction costs, faster project delivery and greater investor confidence.
Ultimately, infrastructure development doesn't happen in isolation. It depends on an ecosystem where transportation works efficiently, businesses have reliable energy and investors have confidence in the operating environment. Strengthening these fundamentals will not only benefit the construction industry but also improve Nigeria's overall competitiveness and economic growth.
Looking ahead, which sectors offer the strongest growth opportunities for Nigeria's construction industry?
When you look at Nigeria's development needs, it's clear that the opportunities for the construction industry are enormous. Our infrastructure deficit remains significant, and with a growing population, rapid urbanisation and increasing demand for modern infrastructure, the need for quality construction has never been greater.
I believe affordable housing will continue to be one of the biggest opportunities. Nigeria's housing deficit, estimated at over 20 million units, means there is sustained demand for residential developments across different income levels. Programmes such as the Federal Government's Renewed Hope Cities and Estates Initiative also present opportunities for developers, contractors, manufacturers and financiers to work together in delivering much-needed housing.
Beyond housing, we're seeing increasing opportunities in transportation infrastructure, renewable energy, industrial parks, healthcare facilities, educational infrastructure, logistics hubs and agro-industrial processing zones. As Nigeria continues to position itself as a major economic hub under the African Continental Free Trade Area, there will also be greater demand for ports, warehouses, manufacturing facilities and supporting infrastructure that can drive regional trade.
Another area with tremendous potential is digital and smart infrastructure. From smart buildings and energy-efficient developments to modern construction technologies and sustainable urban planning, the industry is evolving, and companies that embrace innovation will be better positioned to remain competitive.
Ultimately, the opportunities are there, but unlocking them will require continued investment, policy consistency, improved access to long-term financing and stronger collaboration between government and the private sector. If we get those fundamentals right, I have no doubt that Nigeria's construction industry will remain one of the country's strongest drivers of economic growth, investment and job creation for many years to come.
If given the chance to advise the President and Nigeria's economic management team, what would be your top three policy priorities?
If I had the opportunity to make three recommendations, my first would be to make infrastructure development an even stronger national economic priority. Countries that have achieved rapid industrial growth invested heavily in roads, railways, ports, power, housing and other critical infrastructure because these investments unlock private sector growth, improve productivity and create jobs. Every major infrastructure project has a ripple effect across the economy.
Secondly, I believe Nigeria needs to establish more affordable, long-term financing for infrastructure and housing development. With commercial lending rates remaining high, many developers and construction companies struggle to finance projects or invest in modern equipment and technology. Expanding access to single-digit financing through development finance institutions, strengthening Public-Private Partnerships, and encouraging greater participation by pension funds and capital markets would significantly accelerate infrastructure delivery.
My third recommendation would be to aggressively promote local manufacturing of construction materials and technologies. Nigeria has already demonstrated what is possible through the growth of our cement industry. We can build on that success by providing targeted tax incentives, improving power supply, strengthening industrial infrastructure and supporting manufacturers of steel, aluminium, glass, ceramics, electrical fittings and other building materials. That will reduce our dependence on imports, create jobs, encourage technology transfer and make the construction industry more resilient.
Ultimately, these three priorities - infrastructure investment, affordable long-term financing and stronger local manufacturing - are closely connected. If we continue to make progress in these areas, I believe Nigeria will be well positioned to attract more investment, accelerate industrialisation and build the modern infrastructure needed to support long-term economic growth and improve the quality of life for millions of Nigerians.
Despite rapid technological advancements in the global construction industry, Nigeria has been slow to embrace them. What is driving this slow adoption, and what benefits could wider adoption deliver?
There's no doubt that technology is reshaping the construction industry across the world. Countries like the UAE, Singapore and China are increasingly using technologies such as Building Information Modelling, artificial intelligence, drones, digital twins, prefabrication, modular construction and robotics to improve efficiency, reduce costs and deliver projects faster. Nigeria is making progress, but I believe we still have a long way to go in terms of widespread adoption.
One of the biggest barriers is cost. Many small and medium-sized construction firms simply cannot afford to invest in advanced technologies or the specialised training required to use them effectively. We also face challenges such as limited access to long-term financing, inadequate digital infrastructure and, in some cases, resistance to changing traditional ways of working.
Despite these challenges, the benefits of embracing technology are too significant to ignore. Digital tools improve project planning, reduce design errors, minimise material waste, enhance collaboration among project teams and provide clients with greater visibility into project progress. Technologies like BIM allow architects, engineers and contractors to work from a shared digital model, helping to identify potential issues before construction begins. Drones are improving site surveys and inspections, while AI is supporting project scheduling, risk management and predictive maintenance.
We're also seeing growing opportunities in smart buildings, energy-efficient construction, digital project management systems and modern methods of construction such as prefabrication and modular building, all of which can help address Nigeria's housing and infrastructure needs more efficiently.
I believe government, professional bodies, universities and the private sector all have a role to play in accelerating technology adoption. We need greater investment in digital skills, updated academic curricula, stronger collaboration between industry and educational institutions, and incentives that encourage companies to invest in modern construction technologies.
This is one of the reasons we've dedicated sessions at Mega Project Nigeria 2026 to digital construction, artificial intelligence, BIM and emerging technologies. When industry professionals can see these innovations in action, engage directly with technology providers and learn from global experts, adoption becomes much faster.
The future of construction is undoubtedly digital. Companies that embrace innovation today will be better positioned to deliver projects more efficiently, remain competitive and contribute to building smarter, more sustainable infrastructure for Nigeria's future.
The 10th International Construction, Building Materials and Technologies Exhibition is just weeks away. What are your key objectives for this year's event, who will be participating, and what distinguishes this edition from previous ones?
The 10th edition represents a major milestone for us because it reflects a decade of consistently bringing together stakeholders across the construction value chain. Our primary objective this year is to deepen collaboration between government, investors, manufacturers, developers, contractors, consultants and technology providers in order to accelerate infrastructure development in Nigeria and across West Africa.
We expect participation from federal and state government agencies, real estate developers, construction companies, engineering firms, architects, quantity surveyors, manufacturers of building materials, equipment suppliers, financial institutions, international exhibitors from Asia, Europe, the Middle East, Africa and America, professional associations including The Nigerian Society of Engineers, Nigeria Institution of Quantity Surveyors, Nigerian Institution of Builders, The Nigerian Institution of Architects, Nigerian Institution of Civil Engineers and, more importantly, this edition we are partnering with the Real Estate Developers Association of Nigeria, REDAN, and the Mortgage Bankers Association of Nigeria, MBAN.
This year's edition is different because it goes beyond an exhibition. It includes investment forums, business matchmaking sessions, technology demonstrations, policy dialogues, professional masterclasses and awards recognising outstanding contributions to Nigeria's construction industry over the past decade. We are also placing greater emphasis on smart construction, sustainability, affordable housing, green building technologies and digital transformation. What we are doing differently at this edition is the introduction of the Eko Build Summit on day three; that day will feature stakeholders in the built environment from an ESG point of view. Day one is the opening ceremony with state commissioners of infrastructure engaging with key stakeholders from international finance partners. At the end of this edition, we will have state governments signing MoUs with international partners and other key stakeholders.
How will this forum contribute to the growth of Nigeria's construction industry, benefit key stakeholders and support the country's broader economic development?
The exhibition serves as a marketplace for ideas, investment and innovation. It connects manufacturers directly with buyers, introduces new technologies into the Nigerian market and facilitates partnerships that lead to new projects.
For stakeholders, it provides access to knowledge, business opportunities and international best practices. Contractors can meet suppliers, developers can identify investors, government agencies can engage directly with industry players while young professionals gain exposure to emerging technologies.
For Nigeria's economy, increased investments in construction stimulate manufacturing, logistics, financial services, transport and numerous other sectors. Construction has one of the highest multiplier effects because every building project generates employment across multiple industries. The forum therefore contributes to economic diversification, job creation, technology transfer and increased foreign direct investment.
